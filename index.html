The mechanics of a job search

The Job Search Field Guide

Hi! I am Achille, and if you are here, you might be hard at work on your next career chapter :) What follows is a work-in-progress collection of notes and tips on the daunting, but critical process of finding a new job. I hope you find it useful and share your feedback with me (I plan to update it every now and then).

Work takes up something in the order of eighty thousand hours of a life. A job search is one of the few moments when you get any real say over what those hours are spent on, and it usually turns up at the worst time for thinking clearly: when you're tired of where you are, or you've just been let go, or something has shifted and you can't yet put it into words.

The difficult part of that is working out what you actually want, what you're prepared to trade for it, and what you'd regret. Nobody can hand you that.

What follows is the rest of it. The mechanics of a search are surprisingly large: working out where to look, reading postings properly, rewriting a resume for the fifteenth time, finding the right person to message and then actually messaging them, preparing for interviews, holding your nerve on money, and doing all of it again next week when nobody has replied. Left unstructured, that machinery absorbs almost all your attention, and it absorbs it at precisely the moment you can least afford to hand attention over.

Having a method frees up the part of you that should be thinking about the bigger question. If you know what you do on a Tuesday evening, the search stops being a source of low-grade dread and becomes a process you're running.

There's a second reason to work this way. A search generates a lot of information about you, and most people throw all of it away. Twenty applications, six conversations and three interviews will tell you more about what you want and how you come across than any amount of reflection in the abstract, but only if you write some of it down.

How to use it

The phases run roughly in order, though a real search overlaps them within a few weeks. Read the first phase before anything else. Everything downstream leans on it, and the first module in particular is much easier to act on now than in month three.

  • Each module stands on its own, so jump to what you need using the phase menu at the top.
  • The checkboxes tick in your browser and aren't saved or sent anywhere. Print the page if you want a copy you can keep.
  • The advice here is a starting point. What you learn from your own processes will be more specific and more useful, and the Learning section is how you get at it.

1.1
Before you start

Staying in one piece

This comes first because it's far easier to set up before a search wears you down than to introduce three months in, when you most need it and least feel like it.

Why it matters

Job searching asks you to keep working at something where most of your effort produces no response and almost every decision is made by someone else. It runs for months and it is done, usually, in private. Setting up how you'll handle that is as much a part of the method as anything about resumes.

Some numbers help, mainly because they take the outcome off your shoulders. Senior roles routinely draw several hundred applicants. Searches at that level often run three to six months, sometimes longer, and that's an ordinary result rather than a sign that something is wrong with you.

A large review of the research found two things worth knowing. Effort predicts how many interviews you get, but it doesn't predict whether you end up somewhere good; the quality of how you search does. And the longer a search runs, the more motivation depletes under repeated rejection, which reduces both effort and results. The implication isn't to push harder when it drags. It's to change something, and to look after the person doing the searching.

The harder part is what a long search does to your sense of yourself. Rejections arrive without explanation, or don't arrive at all. Work that took you a weekend disappears into a system that never replies. If you're out of work, financial pressure sits underneath all of it, and the search stops feeling like a project and starts feeling like a verdict handed down weekly. Almost everyone who has done this for more than two months has felt some version of that, and the fact that it's common doesn't make it small.

How to do it

  1. Set weekly targets, not daily ones. Three or four proper applications, five to eight conversations, one sweep, one block on whatever you're building, one review. Weekly targets survive a bad day. Daily ones turn one bad day into a failure.
  2. Batch by activity. Searching in one block, tailoring in another, messages in another. Constantly switching between them is what makes it feel like it never stops.
  3. Give it fixed hours with a real end. Checking your email at eleven at night changes nothing and costs you the sleep you need for the interviews.
  4. Take breaks often rather than long. How frequently you stop matters more than how long you stop for.
  5. Separate the outcome from the judgement. A rejection at screening is information about fit, timing and how many other people applied. Very little of it is information about you. That's much easier to write than to feel, which is why it's worth saying out loud to someone regularly.
  6. Tell people you trust what's happening. Searches are usually conducted in secret, which means the hardest part of your year is invisible to everyone around you. Two or three people who know, and who ask, changes the experience considerably.
  7. Keep one thing in your week that has nothing to do with it and doesn't get cancelled when you're busy or discouraged. Exercise, a class, a standing dinner. It's the thing that reminds you the search isn't your whole life.
  8. If you're not working, plan the money deliberately. Knowing what your runway is, rather than sensing it, turns a constant background fear into a date. That's much easier to think next to.
  9. Reassess when it stalls rather than intensifying. Two months of no responses is a signal to change your targeting, your materials or your channels. It isn't a signal to send more of the same, and it isn't a signal about your worth.
  10. Know your own warning signs. Dread before opening the tracker. Applications sent without reading the posting. Sleep going. Withdrawing from people. When these turn up, a deliberate week off beats a forced month off, and the roles will still be there.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Treating it as a full-time job. It produces volume, poor quality and exhaustion, and the research suggests volume isn't what gets you somewhere good anyway.

Reading rejection as a verdict on you. In a market this crowded it's mostly noise about you and mostly signal about fit and timing.

Going it entirely alone. If this is affecting your sleep, your mood or your relationships for more than a couple of weeks, that's worth talking to someone about. A job search is a stressful life event and it's reasonable to treat it as one.

1.2
Before you start

Decide what you're looking for

One page, written before you look at a single job posting. Everything after this either follows from it or happens by accident.

Why it matters

Searches tend to start reactively. A posting appears, it looks plausible, an application goes out. Three months later there are forty applications, no pattern in them, and no way to tell whether the market is bad or the strategy is.

Writing this down takes an hour and gives you something to check every posting against in about ten seconds. Revisit it monthly. What you change tells you a lot.

How to do it

  1. Name three to five job titles you're competitive for right now, based on what you've been paid to do rather than what you'd enjoy. Add a second tier of adjacent roles you'd take, and a third of genuine stretches you'll apply to selectively.
  2. List the industries you want, in order. Then, separately and honestly, the ones you're open to because that's where the volume is. Both lists are legitimate. Keeping them separate stops you drifting into the second while believing you're pursuing the first.
  3. Write what you're ruling out, specifically enough that it can actually disqualify a real posting. "Bad culture" never fires. "No ownership of what I design" does. Think about what made previous roles wrong and turn those into criteria.
  4. Settle three numbers: what you're targeting, what you'd be delighted by, and the lowest you'd accept for something exceptional. Deciding this calmly now is much easier than deciding it live in a recruiter call.
  5. Note location and working pattern, including anything on a longer horizon that should shape which employers you favour.
  6. List four or five things a role has to give you. These become your interview questions and, later, how you compare offers.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Writing a wish list. If every line is aspirational, nothing filters. This document has to be capable of saying no.

Never opening it again. A brief written once and ignored is worse than none, because it gives you the feeling of having a strategy.

1.3
Before you start

Build one master resume

A long, private document holding everything you've ever done. You never send it. You cut every application resume out of it, which is what makes tailoring fast enough to sustain.

Why it matters

Most people write resume content from scratch for every application, which is why applying is exhausting and why the fifteenth application is so much worse than the first.

Three jobs are usually done at once and shouldn't be. Getting it all down. Choosing what matters for a particular role. Polishing what you chose. The master resume is the first of those, done once. It runs three or four pages and that's correct, because nobody outside your own filing system will ever read it.

How to do it

  1. Facts first. Every organisation, title, and start and end date. Write them down once and stop hunting for them.
  2. Aim for ten to fifteen entries per recent role. Don't edit while you write. If you stop to polish, you'll stall at the second job.
  3. Write achievements, not duties. "Responsible for onboarding" describes a job description. "Rebuilt onboarding for 200 new starters a year and cut time-to-productivity from ten weeks to six" describes you. If a line could appear on the resume of anyone who held that title, it isn't doing any work.
  4. Get the numbers down while you still remember them. Scale, before and after, time or money saved. Where there's no number, write the outcome and who noticed. Never make one up.
  5. Jog your memory by reading job descriptions for roles you already held and asking "did I do that?", and by looking at published resume examples for your titles. Both are much easier than staring at a blank page.
  6. Dump every skill, tool, language and certification. You'll switch these on and off per application.
  7. Keep the formatting plain. A simple document, reverse chronological, no columns, no graphics, no photo. Applicant tracking systems handle ordinary PDFs and Word files fine; two-column layouts and images are where they struggle. Design work has almost no effect on whether you get an interview, and can quietly cost you one.
  8. Add to it the week something happens, not the month you start looking.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Editing while writing. Get it all down badly first. Editing is a separate afternoon.

Letting an AI generate the content. Anything you can't defend under a follow-up question is a liability, and follow-up questions are what interviews are.

1.4
Before you start

Make yourself findable

Your public profile is either working while you sleep or it isn't. Most of what helps sits in settings recruiters see and colleagues don't.

Why it matters

Two things decide whether recruiters contact you: whether you turn up in their searches, and whether your first few lines make them click. Both are closer to a keyword problem than a writing problem.

If you're currently employed, sort every change into what your network can see and what it can't, then start with the second group.

How to do it

  1. Turn on recruiter-facing availability settings. On LinkedIn, "open to work" set to recruiters only stays hidden from your network and from your own employer's recruiters. Fill in target titles, locations, start date and employment type while you're there.
  2. Fix your skills list. Skills feed search ranking and are close to invisible socially. Put the three that match your target roles at the top and add every related term you see in the postings you want.
  3. Lead your headline with your title. Someone scanning search results wants to confirm what kind of candidate you are before they read anything clever. Avoid "seeking opportunities" and "pivoting"; both raise questions before you've said anything.
  4. Load the first three lines of your summary. Seniority, scale, and one result. That's all that shows before the fold.
  5. Be realistic about a portfolio. It rarely helps at the screening stage, because links aren't rendered by applicant systems and screeners spend seconds. It earns its keep afterwards: when someone looks you up, as something to walk through in an interview, and in direct outreach where you set the context.
  6. Line up two or three references early, while relationships are warm and nobody is under time pressure.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Polishing your profile instead of applying. It feels productive and produces very little. Give it an afternoon and stop.

2.1
Finding roles

Where to look

Job boards give you volume. Company career pages give you roles that never reach the boards, or reach them late.

Why it matters

Plenty of roles at good employers surface on the employer's own site first, and get mistagged or buried on aggregators. Running only one channel narrows your search without you noticing.

Timing matters too. A posting that has been open for six weeks has less energy behind it than one posted on Tuesday. Where something is clearly a fit, applying quickly beats another evening of polishing.

How to do it

  1. Collect the vocabulary before you search. The same job carries wildly different titles across organisations. Write down every variant you see on roles you'd take, plus the method words that show up in the postings you like. Search on the set, not on one title.
  2. Set standing alerts on two or three job boards using that vocabulary, filtered by location and recency. Read them in one batch on a fixed day rather than checking constantly. Beyond the general boards, a directory of niche, industry-specific boards is worth a look if your field has one.
  3. Build a list of twenty to thirty employers you actually want, each with a direct link to their careers search. It takes an afternoon once, then twenty minutes a week to sweep.
  4. Sweep weekly. Career pages don't change fast enough to justify daily checking, and daily checking wears you down.
  5. Remember the third channel. Roles reach people through conversations before they reach a board. That's the People section.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Searching one job title. You'll miss most of what's out there.

Checking every day. It costs energy you need elsewhere and surfaces almost nothing new.

2.2
Finding roles

Decide quickly whether it's worth it

Three questions and a verdict, in about two minutes, before you write anything. The point is to keep your good hours for your good chances.

Why it matters

Senior roles routinely draw several hundred applicants. A weak application costs you the same evening as a strong one, so the main thing to get right is which postings you spend evenings on.

How to do it

Three questions, in this order:

  1. Can you honestly claim the requirements? The first three or four listed carry most of the weight; the rest is often a wish list. As a rule of thumb you want most of the list overall and nearly all of the top few. If something central is missing, this probably isn't your posting.
  2. Do you actually want the work? Ask it separately from whether you'd get it. Postings taken on out of anxiety are how people end up somewhere worse than where they started.
  3. Does it clear your brief? Industry, salary, location.

Then one verdict, from three:

  • Worth a real effort. Yes to all three. This gets a proper evening.
  • Worth applying if there's time. A soft no somewhere, but nothing disqualifying. Do it after the first tier is clear.
  • Not worth it. A hard no on any of the three. Record it and move on.

Three tiers is enough. Anything more elaborate turns into admin you won't keep up, and the point of the rating is that you act on it.

Write down the ones you skip. A month of skips is useful evidence about whether your targeting or the market is the problem.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Rating generously because you want it to fit. The verdict is only useful if it can come out low.

Skipping this for roles you're excited about. Those are the ones where enthusiasm hides a missing requirement.

2.3
Finding roles

Keep track

A spreadsheet is enough. What matters is that in two months it can tell you where you're getting stuck.

Why it matters

A tracker earns its keep the week you start asking why nothing is working. One that only records company, role and date can't answer that. One that also records how far you got, and whether you contacted anyone, usually can.

How to do it

  1. One sheet, one row per role. Company, role, location, your rating, salary if published, status, date applied, whether you contacted anyone, how far you got, notes, link.
  2. Three of those columns do the diagnostic work: how far you got, whether you contacted anyone, and your own rating. If you drop anything, keep those.
  3. Keep the statuses simple: to apply, applied, interviewing, closed. Colour them, because colour is what makes it readable at a glance.
  4. Record how far you got on everything that ends. This is the column that tells you which problem you have.
  5. Record outreach honestly, including the messages that got no reply.
  6. Look at it once a week and ask where things are stopping. No response at all points at your materials, your targeting or a location filter. Screening calls that go nowhere point at positioning and the predictable questions. Late rounds lost point at delivery and self-advocacy. These need completely different fixes, and treating one as another wastes months.

If you'd rather not build one from scratch: the same columns, with the dropdowns, the colour coding and a weekly review sheet, ready to fill in.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Recording applications but not conversations. You lose the ability to notice that the channel you've neglected is the one that would have worked.

Making it beautiful. It's an instrument, not a deliverable.

3.1
Applying

Read the posting properly

Ten minutes before you touch your resume. It gives you the words to use, the things to lead with, and a decision you've actually made rather than drifted into.

Why it matters

Tailoring without reading the posting closely is guesswork about what to emphasise. Reading it closely takes ten minutes and saves more than that in the next step.

How to do it

  1. Pull out their exact language. The words and phrases they use, grouped roughly by theme. Use theirs rather than your preferred synonyms. If they say "capability building", don't write "skills development" and hope someone makes the connection.
  2. Work out what the job actually does all day. Strategy or delivery, design or execution, managing stakeholders or building things. One sentence. It decides which of your bullets go first.
  3. Note what looks good to you. Not to anyone else. Pull these straight from your brief: the scope, ownership, methods, mission or working pattern you said mattered. Two people can read the same posting and find opposite things encouraging, and that's fine.
  4. Note what gives you pause. Again, yours. Something that reads as a warning sign to one person is the exact job someone else wants. The useful move is to write down what specifically bothers you and turn it into a question you'll ask if you get an interview.
  5. Write your decision down. Worth real effort, worth applying, or not for you, with a line of reasoning. A decision you have to write is harder to fudge than one you keep in your head.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Skipping this for roles you badly want. Wanting something isn't the same as having read it.

Treating every requirement as equal. The order tells you a lot about what they actually care about.

3.2
Applying

Tailor the resume

Show the 10% of you that is 100% relevant to them. That makes it a selection problem rather than a writing one, which is why it takes fifteen minutes and not an hour.

Why it matters

Resumes get skimmed, then scanned, then read. The skim takes a couple of seconds and looks at titles, employers and dates down the left. The scan hunts for particular words. Only what survives both gets read properly. Order and vocabulary do most of the work here, and prose quality does less than you'd think.

So the job is to show the 10% of you that is 100% relevant to them. No single resume can be uniquely right for every posting you answer, and you can't spend an hour on each and keep going either. Having a master resume is what lets both be true: you're picking the tenth that fits this one, not writing anything new.

How to do it

  1. Put the title you're applying for at the top. It confirms in one second what kind of candidate you are, which matters most when your own titles are unusual or undersell the work.
  2. Rewrite the summary for this role. Three or four lines, using their language. If you tailor nothing else, tailor the title and the summary, because both sit in the part of the page people actually read.
  3. Reorder your bullets. The first one under each role should be the most relevant to this posting, not the most impressive in general. People read downwards and stop early.
  4. Turn entries on and off from the master. Density of relevant material beats completeness.
  5. Make the match explicit. If they ask for cross-functional delivery, a line saying you worked with other departments doesn't prove it. Naming the departments, the thing delivered and how it landed does.
  6. Cut the skills line down to this posting. Fifteen or twenty terms is plenty.
  7. Two pages is fine. The one-page rule is about being brief and legible. Shrinking to six-point type to obey it defeats the point.
  8. Check the exported file. If you can select the text, a machine can read it.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Perfectionism. The fourth revision rarely changes anything. Applying two weeks earlier sometimes does.

Bullets you can't defend. Everything on the page is a question someone might ask you.

3.3
Applying

The application message

For “why are you interested” boxes and short notes to recruiters. Around 200 words, and it should sound like you.

Why it matters

These usually fail the same way, by restating the resume in sentences. The reader already has the resume. What the box is asking for is the part that isn't on it: why you, why here, and what you're like.

How to do it

  1. Draft a straightforward version first, based on what the role needs and what you've done that maps onto it.
  2. Then answer three questions out loud, without editing yourself. What's something from your work you're actually proud of, in human terms rather than metrics? What draws you to this organisation that wouldn't be true of its competitor? How would a colleague describe working with you?
  3. Rewrite using how you said it, not just what you said. The phrasing is the part that reads as a person.
  4. Keep it to 150 to 250 words and open with a reason rather than a summary of yourself.
  5. Delete any sentence that would be equally true of another employer. If very little survives, you're not ready to send it.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Having an AI write the whole thing. The output is fluent, generic, and increasingly obvious to people reading dozens a week.

Praising the mission with no evidence you've engaged with it.

4.1
People

The people who help are the ones you barely know

Not your close colleagues. The people you know slightly. That comes from a five-year randomised experiment rather than from networking folklore.

Why it matters

Researchers ran an experiment on LinkedIn across roughly 20 million people, producing about 600,000 job moves, and tested whether weak connections really do help more than strong ones. They do, with a wrinkle: the relationship is a curve rather than a straight line. Very weak ties aren't the most useful. Moderately weak ones are. The effect was strongest in digital and remote-friendly industries.

The reason is information. People close to you know roughly what you know and move in the same rooms. Someone you worked with briefly four years ago sits in a different flow of information, which is where unlisted roles and honest reads on a company tend to come from.

How to do it

  1. Write down thirty people you know slightly. Former colleagues from two or three jobs back, people from training courses and conferences, ex-clients, people you've exchanged messages with online. Not your closest friends, and not strangers.
  2. Sort them by how close they sit to the employers you want.
  3. Get back in touch before you need anything. Something specific about their work, a line about what you're up to, genuine interest. Eighty words. No ask.
  4. When you do ask, ask something answerable. "Do you know anyone at this company?" or "How does this function actually sit in your organisation?" will get a reply. "Let me know if you hear of anything" asks them to do the thinking, and mostly goes unanswered.
  5. Five to eight real conversations a week is a pace you can hold. Fifty isn't, and the quality falls apart.
  6. Tell people what came of it. It's the cheapest way to become someone people help a second time.

When the list runs out

Thirty names is a good start and it will not last the search. At some point you need people you have no connection to at all, and the instinctive move — messaging everyone at one organisation and then hunting for an email address — is the lowest-yield approach there is, as well as the one that produces the feeling of a closed door. Three better ones.

  • Go via a person. The strongest channel most people have is somebody else making the ask on their behalf, and it's underused because it feels like a larger imposition than it is. Make it small by writing the message for them — three or four sentences they can forward without rewriting, so introducing you costs one line and the other person can decline without anyone being embarrassed. Something like: "[Name] is a [what you do] who's spent the last few years on [area]. They're looking into [field] at the moment — not job hunting, working out whether it's the right direction — and wanted to talk to someone who actually does it. Would you be up for twenty minutes? Happy to pass on their details."
  • Pick people the platforms don't surface. Speakers at conferences and meetups. Podcast guests. Named authors of the field's reports. Community Slacks and Discords. Course and programme cohorts. Alumni directories, which exist for exactly this and are barely used.
  • Join something rather than approaching people one at a time. A working group, a course, a volunteer project, a regular meetup — it turns cold approaches into ordinary conversations with people you now have a reason to know. Slower to start, much cheaper to sustain, and usually the better investment if you're exploring rather than chasing a particular job.

Two groups have unusual reasons to say yes. People eighteen months into a job still remember what they wished they'd known and are flattered to be asked. People who have recently left will tell you things nobody currently employed there will.

Send in batches. Eight or ten in one sitting, coming back over a fortnight. Sent one at a time, each carries the full weight of a possible no; sent together, no single reply means much.

If it feels grubby

Deliberately building a connection because it might be useful produces a measurable sense of impurity, and it's worst for whoever has less power in the exchange — which, if you're the one asking, is you. It isn't a flaw in your character and willpower doesn't touch it.

The usual remedy — bring them something in return — often isn't available and shouldn't be forced. Someone crossing into a new field has little to offer a stranger fifteen years into it, and a manufactured gift reads exactly as manufactured. What removes the feeling is dropping the pretence instead. The discomfort comes from the gap between an instrumental purpose and a social wrapper, so close the gap: say plainly what you're doing and why you're asking them in particular. Openly instrumental is comfortable for everybody; instrumental dressed as friendly is what makes both parties uneasy. The two things you always have are worth more than they look — your full attention, and telling them afterwards what came of it. If a real favour presents itself later, do it. Don't invent one now.

Worth reading

80,000 Hours, How to network — free, unusually good, and written for people exploring rather than applying. The source of the five-minute favour, and of the argument for joining a community over approaching people one by one.

Casciaro, Gino and Kouchaki, Learn to Love Networking, Harvard Business Review, 2016 — the research behind the section above, in four pages.

Squiggly Careers, An Introvert's Guide to Networking — twenty minutes, practical, and the least off-putting treatment of this going. The Four People Every Squiggly Career Network Needs is the companion.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

"Let me know if you hear of anything." Almost nobody acts on this, because it isn't a question.

Only contacting people when you need them. It's obvious from the outside, and it burns the connection for next time.

Staying inside your close circle because it feels safer. That's the group the research says helps least.

Going straight at the target. Messaging ten people at one company is worse than one forwarded message from somebody who knows one of them.

4.2
People

Exploring a field

Twenty to thirty minutes with someone already doing work you're curious about, when you don't yet know whether it's for you. You're researching, not auditioning, and you may be six months from applying to anything.

Why it matters

Two quite different conversations go by the name "informational interview", and most advice is written for the other one — the kind you have once you know the shape of what you want and are after routes in, which is 4.3. This one is for when you don't know: what the work consists of, what it pays, how your experience translates once it crosses the border, whether you'd want the day-to-day. Run as though it were the other kind, it feels like a failure — you come off a good call with no lead and conclude it went nowhere.

So say which one you're in, to yourself and to them. "I'm not looking for a role at the moment, I'm trying to work out whether this is a field I should be in" changes the other person's behaviour immediately: they stop assessing you and start explaining their world, which is both more useful to you and something people enjoy far more.

These conversations rarely produce a job, and that isn't a fault in the method. What one reliably produces is better language and one or two more names; the offer, when it comes, arrives sideways, out of the twentieth conversation, from someone you spoke to in March. Judged one at a time the return looks poor, which is why most people stop early. Judged as twenty over two months, it's the highest-yielding thing in this guide.

What you're collecting

You're calibrating two things against each other: what the field is really like, and what you look like from inside it. Postings won't tell you either — they describe idealised roles in the field's own shorthand. Five things worth coming away with:

  • What the work consists of when nobody's describing it for an advert
  • What it's called — titles, the functions next door, the words used for what you already do
  • What's genuinely paid for, as against admired but unfunded
  • How your background reads from over there, and which parts they'd need talking into
  • What's changing, and so what gets hired for in two years rather than last year

The second one matters more than it looks. You can't search for work you can't name, or tell whether a posting fits when the vocabulary is foreign. Most people crossing into a new field are missing the language rather than the ability, and it's only available from people already using it — which means these conversations are where it comes from, and you don't need it beforehand.

How to do it

  1. Work out how many attempts you'll make before you judge whether it's working. Cold approaches to strangers get a reply perhaps one time in five; a warm route does several times better. A run of silences is the ordinary texture of a working process rather than information about you — obvious written down in advance, and not at all obvious after the fifth no-reply, which is where people stop and mistake the stopping for a finding.
    • Log the two failures separately. Silence is a targeting problem — wrong person, wrong channel, nothing anyone can act on. A call that happens and goes flat is a framing problem — the ask was too vague to prepare for. Left unseparated, a run of silences turns into a belief about how you land on people, which is much harder to shift than a bad channel.
  2. Pick people, not job titles. Someone doing the work, someone a level above it, and someone who left it. The third is usually the most informative and the least contacted. 4.1 covers where to find them when you have no connection at all.
  3. Ask for advice, not for time. "Twenty minutes of your time" requests a resource; "you're one of the few people who'd actually know" requests a judgment, which is easier to grant and more pleasant to be asked for. Say why you're in touch, what you're exploring, and offer whatever time suits them. Under a hundred words.
  4. Make the ask specific, not yourself. The specificity has to live somewhere, and it's easier to put in the question than in your plans. "Could I pick your brain about the field" asks a stranger to work out what you need. "What's changed in how teams like yours are staffed in the last couple of years?" is answerable by someone who knows nothing about you at all — which is exactly the condition you're in. Vague about where you're heading is fine. Vague about what you're asking is what kills it.
    • If you want a ready-made set, the TIARA questions from The 2-Hour Job Search are built for exploring rather than applying — trends, insights, assignments, resources, advice, roughly in that order, so that the opening question invites them to talk as an expert and the advice comes last, once there's enough context for it to be worth anything. The questions, written out.
  5. Don't ask for a job. You aren't ready to take one, and asking converts a generous conversation into an awkward one. If they raise an opening, follow it. If they don't, leave it.
  6. Close with two questions. Is there anything I should be reading, and is there anyone else I should talk to. The second is how a conversation becomes a network.
  7. Write down the words afterwards — titles, phrases, the names of things. That list is the deliverable, and in three months it's what lets you read a posting properly.
  8. Say thank you within a day, referring to something specific they said, and tell them later if it led somewhere. Almost nobody does this, and it's where most of the value ends up.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Waiting until you can describe yourself properly. The conversations are where the description comes from. Postponing them until you're articulate is preparation that never ends.

Turning it into a pitch. People feel the switch immediately and the conversation closes over.

Stopping after six. Not enough to tell you anything, and stopping gets mistaken for a finding.

Worth reading

Herminia Ibarra, Working Identity — the book behind the claim that you act your way into clarity rather than thinking your way into it. Worth it for anyone who suspects they ought to have it worked out before they start talking to people.

4.3
People

Conversations at a company you're targeting

The same twenty minutes, different stakes. Here you know where you'd like to work, which makes the conversation informational in form and evaluative in effect whether or not anyone says so.

Why it matters

The mechanism isn't the referral you ask for. It's the opinion that already exists when your name comes up. If you apply three weeks later, whoever you spoke to will quite likely be asked what they made of you, and will answer whether or not you ever requested anything. That is the whole game here: be someone worth mentioning, rather than someone who extracted an introduction.

Before you apply is worth far more than after. Same message, different reading — beforehand you're a person with a question, afterwards you're a candidate chasing a decision. The conversation also gets you the thing postings never contain: what the team is currently stuck on. Knowing that changes how you'd write the application entirely.

How to do it

  1. Go one step off the hiring line. The hiring manager is the most guarded person you could pick. A peer of the role, or someone in an adjacent team, will speak more freely — and is often exactly who gets asked "do you know anyone?"
  2. Do the reading first. Ask nothing the careers page, the product, or the last six months of their announcements would answer. The cost of a lazy question is higher here than anywhere else in this phase, because you are being assessed.
  3. Ask about the problem, not the opening. What does the team keep running into? What would have to be true for the next year to go well? What does good look like here that wouldn't look good somewhere else? These get you real answers and they are also, incidentally, the questions that make people think you'd be useful.
  4. State your interest once, plainly, and then stop. One sentence — "I'd apply if something opened up that fitted" — puts it on the record and asks them for nothing. Don't ask for a referral on a first conversation. It's the most common way to spend a connection before it's worth anything, and it puts someone who has met you once in an uncomfortable position.
  5. If they raise a role, ask what a strong application would show. You'll get the hiring bar described in plain language, which is not something the posting will do.
  6. Follow up with something, not just thanks. The thing you went and read because they mentioned it; a question their answer raised. That's what makes a second conversation possible, and second conversations are where referrals actually come from.
  7. Talk to more than one person there. One person's read on a company is one person's read, and you're about to make a decision on it.
  8. If you apply later, tell them — and don't ask them to do anything. Most people help at that point without being asked. The ones who don't weren't going to.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Asking for a referral from someone who has met you once. They either say no and it's awkward, or say yes and it's worthless, because a referral is only as good as what the referrer can say about you.

Performing. Treating it as a pre-interview and answering rather than asking. People notice, and it throws away the one advantage of the format — you can ask things a candidate can't.

One person, one company, one read. Enthusiasm from a single well-placed conversation is thin evidence about a place you'd spend years.

Going quiet after they help. The most reliable way to make a helpful person unhelpful next time.

4.4
People

Recruiters, hiring managers and referrals

Short messages to named people, sent around the time you apply. Uncomfortable, cheap, and it puts a person next to a row in a database.

Why it matters

Applications go into a queue. Messages go to someone. The message doesn't have to be good, it has to be short, specific and obviously not a template.

A referral does something slightly different: it gets your application read. That's most of its value, and it doesn't need to come from anyone senior.

How to do it

  1. Find two people. The recruiter named on the posting or visible in the talent team, and whoever the role probably reports to. The posting's language usually gives away the second.
  2. Write four sentences. Who you are, the one thing in your background that meets their hardest requirement, one specific thing about their team or work, and an easy close. Under 120 words.
  3. Write the two versions differently. The recruiter cares about whether you clear the bar. The hiring manager cares about what you'd take off their plate. They talk to each other.
  4. Don't attach anything unless you're asked to. It reads as a mass send.
  5. Follow up once, a week or so later, adding something new rather than repeating yourself. Then stop.
  6. For referrals, do the work for them. Send the link, two or three sentences they can paste, and a genuine way to say no without it being awkward. Be honest about how well they know you rather than overstating it. If the connection is too thin for a referral, ask them something else instead.
  7. Use the company's referral route rather than applying separately and asking someone to chase it.
  8. Tell whoever helped what happened. People who never hear the outcome don't help twice.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Sending the same message to both people.

Following up four times. After the second message it reads as pressure.

Asking a near-stranger for a referral. It puts them somewhere uncomfortable and rarely produces a real one.

5.1
Interviews

Preparing

Early, out loud, and tested by someone else. Most preparation is late, silent and unexamined, which is a scheduling problem more than an effort one.

Why it matters

Work done the night before is done tired. Work done alone is never tested. Work read silently is never timed, so you find out in the room that your answer runs four minutes. None of these are about how hard you're trying.

How to do it

  1. Book the preparation when you book the interview, as two or three short sessions across the week rather than one long evening.
  2. Write and rehearse the predictable answers. Your introduction, a weakness, a failure, a disagreement with a colleague, feedback that was hard to hear, why this role. These come up constantly and there's no reason to meet them cold.
  3. Read the company properly. Recent news, how they make money, what's changing, what the role probably exists to fix.
  4. Look up who you're meeting. Their background tells you what they'll care about and what will go over their head.
  5. Do one run-through out loud, against a clock, in front of someone who'll be honest, several days ahead rather than the night before. Reading your notes builds familiarity. Speaking builds fluency, and only speaking shows you how long you actually take.
  6. Prepare your own questions, and make them ones the website can't answer.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Being caught by the weakness question. It's one of the most predictable questions there is and it's answerable in advance.

Preparing silently. You'll discover the length problem in the room.

5.2
Interviews

Your stories, and the STAR structure

Six well-built stories will answer most behavioural questions. Thirty half-built ones answer none, because you can't retrieve any of them under pressure.

Why it matters

Behavioural interviews ask you to describe things you've actually done, on the theory that past behaviour predicts future behaviour better than opinions do. The standard structure for answering is STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. It's used widely enough that many interviewers are scoring against it, and it's a useful discipline in any case, because it forces you to say what you personally did and how it turned out.

The skill worth building is reshaping. One story about a stalled project can answer questions about conflict, influence, failure, stakeholders or resilience depending on which part you bring forward, but only if you know it well enough to move the emphasis around.

How to do it

  1. Pick six stories with range. A clear success with a number attached, a real failure, a disagreement you handled, something you got done without authority, something you built from nothing, and a time you changed your mind.
  2. Write each one in four parts. Situation in two sentences. Task, meaning what was on you specifically. Action, in the first person singular, three or four steps. Result, with a number if one exists and an honest outcome if not.
  3. Say "I" for the actions. "We redesigned the process" tells an interviewer nothing about you. It's the most common way a good story stops working.
  4. Rehearse each to about two minutes, out loud, timed.
  5. Map each story to the questions it can cover. One story should stretch to three or four.
  6. Make the failure story a real failure. A real cost, and a specific thing you do differently now. Failures that are secretly successes are obvious, and they cost you credibility on everything else you've said.
  7. Keep a note after each interview of what you were asked and how it landed, and feed it back in.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Saying "we" throughout. Interviewers can't assess a team.

Stories without an ending. If you can't say what happened, it isn't an answer.

5.3
Interviews

Case studies and presentations

A brief, a deadline, and a room. What's being assessed is how you think and how you land it, more than whether you arrive at one particular answer.

Why it matters

These rounds exist because a conversation can't show how someone handles an open problem. The panel is watching you scope something, decide what matters, back a view, and stay coherent under questioning.

The main constraint is time. A brief that would take a team a month is being answered in thirty minutes, which makes choosing what to leave out part of the exercise.

How to do it

  1. Clarify the brief before you build anything. Ask the recruiter about the format, how long you have, who'll be in the room, whether they want slides, and how much of it is presentation versus discussion. These questions are welcome, and the answers change what you make.
  2. Decide what you're going deep on, and say so. "There's a lot in this brief, so I've gone deep on two things and I'll touch the rest briefly" tells the room you prioritised rather than ran out of time.
  3. Put the answer near the front. What you'd do, why, and roughly in what order. People follow an argument better when they know where it's going, and if you run out of time you've already said the important part.
  4. Say what you're assuming. You don't have the internal information they have. Naming an assumption out loud is more convincing than hedging, and it lets someone correct you cheaply.
  5. Ground it in things you've actually seen work. A specific example from your experience does more than a framework, particularly with a mixed panel where not everyone shares your professional background.
  6. Time it and cut. These almost always over-run. Leave real room for questions, because the discussion is often where the decision gets made.
  7. Expect challenge and welcome it. Know the three or four hardest questions someone could put to you, and be willing to say "that's a fair point" and adjust. Holding a position and updating a position are both good signals. Refusing to do either isn't.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Trying to cover the whole brief. There isn't time, and the attempt reads as an inability to prioritise.

Explaining your method instead of your answer. Method is worth showing underneath a recommendation rather than instead of one.

Building for a specialist audience when the room is mixed.

5.4
Interviews

On the day

Things that are hard to fix by preparing more, and reasonably easy to practise.

Why it matters

Interviews are conversations held under artificial conditions. Most of what goes wrong on the day is about length, honesty and attention rather than knowledge.

How to do it

  1. Aim for about two minutes an answer. Make the point, give one example, stop. If they want more they'll ask, and the pause afterwards is theirs to fill rather than yours.
  2. Watch for signs they have enough. Interviewers usually signal it before they say it. Finishing cleanly is better than continuing until someone interrupts.
  3. Say when you don't know. "I haven't hit exactly that, but here's how I'd approach it" is more convincing than stretching a half-relevant example, and experienced interviewers can tell the difference. Taking a couple of seconds to think is fine, and worth saying out loud.
  4. Listen to the whole question. Answering the question you expected rather than the one you were asked is common and very visible.
  5. Adjust if something isn't working. If an answer clearly missed, say so and try again. Correcting yourself reads as responsiveness.
  6. Ask your questions. What would make the first year a success, what's hardest about this role that isn't in the description, what's been tried before and didn't work.
  7. Handle the basics. Test the link, have water, have a notebook, know who you're meeting, leave a buffer either side. Fatigue and logistics account for more bad interviews than anyone admits.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Talking past the end of your answer. Usually a worry that it wasn't enough.

Deciding it's too late to change course. It isn't.

5.5
Interviews

Money and scope

Decide your numbers before the first call and say them when you're asked. Silence is generally taken as agreement.

Why it matters

These conversations get put off precisely when things are going well. The recruiter is friendly, the process is moving, and raising a constraint feels like it might spoil something, so the number goes unchallenged and you carry on at a figure you've quietly stopped being pleased about.

Bands flex more often than candidates assume, and level is sometimes negotiable when the alternative is losing someone they've spent six weeks assessing. A warm, direct question costs very little.

How to do it

  1. Have three numbers before the first call. Your target, what would delight you, and the point below which you'd say no. Written down.
  2. Find out the real range for this title, company size and location. Many places now publish ranges by law, so use those, including for comparable employers.
  3. Give a range whose bottom you'd take. Being vague doesn't preserve leverage, it just hands the decision to them.
  4. If their ceiling is below your floor, say so there and then. "That's below where I need to be, is there any flexibility, or should we be honest that this might not work?" No ultimatum, no apology. This is the sentence people skip.
  5. Negotiate more than salary. Title, scope, team, budget, reporting line, start date, leave, flexibility, learning budget. Several of these move more easily than base pay and matter more over three years.
  6. Get the offer in writing before you resign anything.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Letting a low number pass because the conversation is pleasant. It's generally heard as agreement.

Carrying on while quietly disengaging. It shows up in how you turn up for the rounds that follow.

6.1
Learning

Write it down afterwards

Within a day, before the memory turns into a verdict. A search produces a lot of evidence and very little learning, because almost nobody writes any of it down.

Why it matters

Within a few days an interview compresses into "that went well" or "I made a mess of it", and everything usable disappears. The specific question that landed badly, the moment the room changed, the thing you wish you'd said. Twenty minutes while it's fresh keeps all of it.

Do it after the ones that go well too. Those tell you what to repeat.

How to do it

  1. Write four things. What you were asked. What you said. What you wish you'd said. What surprised you.
  2. Separate what you said from how you said it. A weak answer and a good answer delivered badly are different problems, and the fix for one does nothing for the other.
  3. Note anything they pushed on twice. If there's another round, going away and closing that specific gap is one of the most useful things you can do, and panels notice when someone has.
  4. Ask for feedback when a process ends. "I'd welcome anything that would help me in future processes" gets more than asking why you didn't get it. Sometimes you'll get nothing, which is normal and not a comment on you.
  5. Record how far you got. Over a few months that column tells you which part of your search needs work.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Only writing up the rejections. The ones that went well carry the instructions.

Writing a verdict instead of a record. "It went badly" is no use to you next month.

6.2
Learning

Find your own patterns

A short document holding what your own processes have taught you. It's the difference between ten interviews and the same interview ten times.

Why it matters

Individual write-ups are raw material. What you want out of them is the small number of things that keep happening across different companies, panels and roles. Those are specific to you, and they're the actual curriculum of your search.

This matters more than it might sound. General advice about interview mistakes is easy to agree with and hard to act on. Something you've watched yourself do three times, described in your own words, is much harder to ignore, and it's what you'll actually check before the next round. Most people are carrying three or four of these at most.

How to do it

  1. After a process ends, move anything that would happen again from the write-up into this document. Most of a debrief is specific to that day and stays there.
  2. Group it by situation. Screening calls, behavioural rounds, presentations, panels, money conversations. Then you can read only the relevant part before an interview.
  3. Name each pattern in your own words, and count how often it's appeared. The count is what gives it weight. Your phrasing is what makes it stick.
  4. Keep a section for what works. Things that got praised are worth using deliberately rather than by accident.
  5. Read the relevant section before every interview. That's the whole mechanism. A document nobody rereads is a diary.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Putting everything in it. Only move across what would happen again somewhere else.

Borrowing someone else's patterns. Advice from a book or a guide is a hypothesis to check against your own record. Yours are the ones your write-ups keep producing.

6.3
Learning

Keep building something

Applications and waiting is a bad diet. One small thing you're making gives you somewhere for effort to go that doesn't depend on anyone else's decision.

Why it matters

Your applications generate a precise list of things employers keep asking for that you can't currently claim. That's a better curriculum than any general advice about upskilling.

There's a second reason, which is that a search made only of applications and silence wears people down quickly. Something you're building is the part of the week where effort reliably produces a result.

How to do it

  1. Look back at the last ten postings you wanted and find the requirements you couldn't claim. Two or three will keep recurring.
  2. Separate can't-do from can't-prove. Some gaps are real capability. Others are things you can do but have no evidence for, which is a much cheaper problem to solve.
  3. Build the smallest thing that would close the gap. Something made rather than something consumed: a prototype, a written analysis, a small piece of freelance or volunteer work. Under twenty hours. Something you could be questioned about.
  4. Put it somewhere people can see it. Work nobody can find doesn't change how you're read.
  5. Feed it back into your resume, profile and stories. A new capability that never reaches your materials doesn't exist to anyone assessing you.
  6. Stay in circulation. Communities, meetups, conferences. They give you knowledge, loose connections, and the feeling of still being a practitioner rather than a candidate.

Done when

Where this goes wrong

Collecting courses. One small thing you made is stronger evidence than a stack of certificates.

Using it to avoid applying. Learning feels productive and carries no risk of rejection. Watch the ratio.
